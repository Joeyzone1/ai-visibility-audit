Calibrating a bench balance

A bench balance drifts with temperature and with the age of its load cell. In a room held at twenty degrees the drift is small enough to ignore for a week, but a bench beside a window will move far enough in a single afternoon to matter for anything weighed to four decimal places. The load cell itself ages: the strain gauge creeps under sustained load, and a balance left with a heavy sample on the pan overnight will read low the next morning.

Why drift happens

Two causes dominate. Thermal expansion changes the geometry of the lever system, and creep in the strain gauge changes its resting resistance. The first is reversible within minutes of the room settling. The second is not, which is why a balance that has been loaded for a long period needs a longer warm-up and a fresh zero before it can be trusted again.

How often to recalibrate

For routine work, once a week against a certified mass is enough. For anything reported to a regulator, calibrate at the start of every session and record the result, because an uncalibrated reading is not evidence. Keep the certificate with the instrument rather than in a drawer, so the person using it can see when it was last checked.

What to record

Record the mass used, the reading, the ambient temperature and the time. A log of four numbers is enough to show drift as a trend rather than as a surprise, and it turns a failed calibration into a diagnosis instead of a mystery.
